# AI writing pattern catalog

The full reference behind the hard rules in SKILL.md. Read it before a proofreading pass, a deep rewrite, or any long piece of fresh writing.

Contents: why AI writing reads as AI; A. performed depth; B. uniformity; C. risk-averse vagueness; D. over-formatting; E. word and phrase flags; overcorrection.

## Why AI writing reads as AI

Model output has statistical fingerprints, and readers have learned them. One recognized tell can taint a whole piece: the reader stops trusting that a person meant what was said. Nearly every tell comes from one of four habits:

1. Performing depth instead of providing it: rhetorical drama that gestures at insight and adds no information, such as fake reveals, false contrasts and inflated stakes.
2. Uniformity: the same sentence rhythm, the same paragraph shape, triads everywhere, every section cut from one template. Human writing is uneven.
3. Risk-averse vagueness: safe abstract words, hedges, unnamed authorities, balanced non-positions, and generalities where a specific would do.
4. Over-formatting: bullets, bold labels, headers and emoji doing the work sentences should do.

Learn these four and you will catch patterns this file never lists. Everything below is one of them in a specific form.

Density decides. Almost any pattern here, used once, can appear in good human writing. The fingerprint is clustering: several tropes together, or one trope repeated. Judge by density, and hold your own output to the same standard while you edit.

## A. Performed depth

### Negative parallelism

Hard rule 2. It comes in several shapes: "It's not just X, it's Y." "This isn't about X. It's about Y." "Not because X, but because Y." "The issue isn't X; the real issue is Y." A dramatic "X, not Y." fragment. X and Y usually describe the same thing, so the sentence adds nothing. State the point once, directly.

- Before: "This isn't just a pricing change, it's a rethink of who our customers are."
- After: "The new prices are aimed at smaller teams than we sold to before."

### Fake suspense

Hard rule 3. "Here's the kicker", "Here's the thing", "But there's a catch", a question answered at once ("The result? Devastating."), or a countdown ("Not a patch. Not a rewrite. A rethink."). When the point is strong, deliver it plainly. When it only works with the buildup, it was weaker than it looked.

### Stakes inflation

Everything is transformative, game-changing, or reshaping an industry. State what changed, at its real size: "Reviews come back in hours instead of days" says more than "fundamentally transforming collaboration".

### Superficial significance clauses

A trailing participle that attaches unearned meaning: "...highlighting its importance", "...underscoring the need for change", "...reflecting broader trends", "...paving the way for innovation", "...cementing its status". Delete the clause. If the significance is real, give it a sentence of its own, with evidence.

### Copula dodges

"Serves as", "stands as", "acts as", "represents" or "marks" where "is" belongs. Use "is". Plain verbs read as human.

### The patronizing analogy

"Think of it as...", "It's like a..." for an idea that needed no metaphor. Cut it. When the idea truly needs a metaphor, use one, once.

### The overworked metaphor

One metaphor stretched across a whole piece. A metaphor may appear once, do its job, and leave.

### Invented concept labels

A coined, analytical-sounding term ("the supervision paradox", "workload creep") cited as if it were an established idea. Make the argument instead of naming it.

### Declared clarity

"The reality is...", "The real story is...", "History is clear on this": asserting that something is clear instead of showing it. Show the evidence and let the reader conclude.

### False vulnerability

Performed honesty or self-awareness: "And yes, since we're being honest...", "I'll admit something most consultants won't." It is a polished, risk-free confession that imitates candor. An honest admission names something particular and costs the writer a little. Cut the performance, and never manufacture an admission the author did not make (rule zero).

### Utopian invitations

"Imagine a world where..." followed by the benefits that arrive once the reader grants the premise. Argue in the present tense: describe what the thing does today, not the future it implies.

### The pedagogical voice

"Let's dive in", "Let's walk through this", "Let's break it down": a teacher's voice for readers who asked for no lesson, even in writing for experts. Cut the invitation and present the material.

### Borrowed-authority lists

A rapid run of famous companies or past revolutions offered as proof: "The printing press, the steam engine, the internet: every one of them was dismissed at first." It is common in technical writing. Use at most one comparison that truly maps, or let the argument stand on its own.

## B. Uniformity

### Tricolon abuse

"Faster, smarter, and more scalable." One triad in a piece is rhetoric; a triad in every paragraph is a fingerprint, especially when the third item restates the second in grander words. Keep the one or two items that are true and distinct.

### Anaphora abuse

Three or more sentences opening with the same words ("They assume... They assume... They assume..."). Keep two at most and restructure the rest.

### Drumbeat fragments

"Short. Punchy. Fragments." and one-line paragraphs for manufactured punch. A single fragment can land; a pattern of them is a tell. Let the rhythm vary naturally.

### Flat rhythm

Sentence after sentence of the same length and shape. Mix them on purpose: put a five-word sentence next to a thirty-word one that winds through a subordinate clause before it lands. Read the passage in your head, and break it up if it sounds like a metronome.

### Disguised lists

"The first challenge is... The second challenge is... The third..." This is prose built on a numbered list's skeleton. Write an honest list, or write connected prose.

### Fractal summaries

The intro says what the piece will say, the sections say it, the outro says what was said, and every paragraph ends with a small bow. Say it once and trust the reader. A paragraph may end on a fact.

### Signposted conclusions

Hard rule 4. "In conclusion", "To sum up", "In summary", or "Ultimately," opening the last paragraph. End on a concrete point. An ending that works needs no announcement.

### Formulaic titles

"X: Why Y Matters", "X: How Y Changes Z". A title can just say the thing.

### Acknowledge, then dismiss

"Despite these challenges..." followed by optimism the text has not earned. When the problems are real, let them weigh what they weigh.

### One-point dilution

One thesis restated ten ways, with different metaphors and framings, to feel thorough. Keep the version that says it best; length follows content. In a rewrite, this is where real cuts come from. In fresh writing, stop when the argument is made.

### Content duplication

Sentences or whole sections repeated word for word, or nearly, in a long piece. Scan anything long for repeats and keep one.

## C. Risk-averse vagueness

### Hedge filler

"It's worth noting that", "It's important to note", "Importantly", "Notably", "Interestingly", "Arguably", "generally speaking", "to some extent". Delete the filler. Hedges that carry meaning stay (rule zero).

### Vague attribution

"Experts say", "studies show", "many believe", "industry observers note". Source-count inflation belongs here too: "several publications have cited" when it means two, or one person's view presented as consensus. Name the source or drop the appeal to authority. Keep the underlying claim when it stands on its own, and count honestly.

### Generic abstractions

Claims that would fit any company, product or topic: "We deliver innovative solutions that drive results." Replace them with the specific when the source material or the conversation supplies one. Never invent one (rule zero); without a specific, keep the claim modest and plain.

### Both-sides mush

A balanced non-position where the evidence supports a stance. When the material supports a conclusion, state it plainly.

### False ranges

"From startups to enterprises, from strategy to execution" where no real spectrum exists. Name the two things plainly ("startups and enterprises"), or cut.

### Flattering setups

"Whether you're X or Y..." is a setup that flatters the audience and says nothing. Cut it and address the actual reader.

## D. Over-formatting

Formatting norms depend on the medium: a README tolerates more structure than a blog post, and deck copy is bullets by nature. Within the medium's norms:

### Bold-first bullets

Hard rule 5. Bullets that open with a bold label and a colon, such as a bolded "Speed:" followed by "builds run 3x faster", and their prose cousin, the paragraph that opens with a bolded claim. This is the most recognizable formatting tell, common in decks and READMEs. Write full-sentence bullets or plain prose, or restructure.

### Bullets doing prose's job

Three connected thoughts need two good sentences, not a list. Keep lists for things that really enumerate: steps, specifications, options.

### Header inflation

A level-two heading every two paragraphs in an 800-word piece. Short pieces usually need few headings or none.

### Scattered bold

Key phrases bolded mid-sentence all through the text. Bold almost nothing.

### Emoji decoration

Emoji in headings, bullets or section labels. Leave them out unless the author's own style uses them.

### Unicode furniture

Arrows (U+2192), curly quotes (U+201C, U+201D) in plain-text contexts, and box-drawing flourishes. Type what a keyboard types.

### Title case everywhere

Every heading in Title Case. Sentence case reads more human in most contexts.

## E. Word and phrase flags

These are flags, not bans. One "crucial" is fine; "crucial", "pivotal" and "vital" on one page is a fingerprint. When a plainer word carries the meaning, use it. Keep the domain terms the audience expects: "robust" in a statistics paper is a technical term.

- Inflated verbs: delve, leverage, utilize, harness, streamline, foster, empower, unlock, elevate, supercharge, revolutionize, spearhead, showcase, underscore, bolster, navigate (as a metaphor), embark.
- Inflated adjectives: robust, seamless, cutting-edge, game-changing, transformative, pivotal, crucial, vital, holistic, intricate, vibrant, dynamic, comprehensive, innovative, ever-evolving.
- Ornate nouns: tapestry, landscape (abstract), realm, journey (as a metaphor), ecosystem (as a metaphor), paradigm, synergy, framework (as decoration), beacon, testament, cornerstone, powerhouse, game-changer.
- Gravitas adverbs: seamlessly, effortlessly, quietly (for weight), deeply, fundamentally, remarkably, truly, genuinely, certainly.
- Stock connectives: "Moreover", "Furthermore" and "Additionally" opening paragraphs; "When it comes to"; "At the end of the day"; "That being said"; "In the world of"; "a wide range of"; "It comes as no surprise".
- Stock phrases (hard rule 6): "I hope this email finds you well", "stands as a testament", "plays a vital role", "in today's fast-paced world", "let's dive in", "without further ado", "look no further".
- Suspense openers (hard rule 3): "Here's the kicker", "Here's the deal", "Here's where things get interesting", "Here's what nobody tells you".
- Plain words that usually win: use, help, show, make, build, start, big, main; and "is" in place of any dodge.

## Overcorrection

The cure for AI prose is not a new uniform. Watch for these while editing:

- All-choppy minimalism: every sentence short and stark is a costume too, and readers have started to recognize it.
- Forced casualness: slang and lowercase pasted onto formal content reads as fake. Competent formal writing is human too; the register belongs to the author and the medium.
- Edits for their own sake: a passage with no tells needs none of your fingerprints. Leaving text alone is a valid edit.
